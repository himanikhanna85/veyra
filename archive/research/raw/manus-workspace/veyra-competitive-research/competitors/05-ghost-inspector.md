# Ghost Inspector — codeless recorded browser testing

## Active identity and intended customer

Ghost Inspector is an active SaaS product for **codeless automated browser testing of websites and web applications**. Its current documentation is still receiving dated browser-version and enterprise-security updates in 2026, and its Chrome recorder listing was updated on 25 August 2026, so this is not an apparently abandoned product. The product remains branded Ghost Inspector; SureSwift Capital announced its acquisition of the business on 12 July 2022. The acquisition announcement describes Ghost Inspector as founded in 2014 and aimed at lowering the technical barrier that otherwise restricts automated testing to engineers. [1] [2] [3]

The intended customer is a team that wants repeatable end-to-end web regression/monitoring without building and maintaining Selenium/Cypress infrastructure: QA testers, engineers who need CI/API hooks, product managers, and smaller or less technical teams. Vendor material explicitly positions it for developers, QA leads and product managers, while its recorder is aimed at turning a manually demonstrated flow into a cloud-executable test. This is a vendor-marketed audience claim, supported by the documented getting-started flow and the product’s extension listing; it is not an independent market-size finding. [4] [5] [6]

## Distinctive capabilities and mechanism

The core mechanism is record-and-replay. A browser extension records clicks, form fills and navigation as a user walks through a site, syncs the recording to Ghost Inspector, and the cloud runner later opens a browser and replays the sequence. The author can switch between recording operations and recording assertions during the same session. The editor then exposes each step as a block that can be added, removed, reordered by drag-and-drop, annotated, made optional, and edited without code. This is a relatively distinctive, polished packaging of the category baseline (recorded browser automation plus a hosted runner), rather than a proven exclusive capability. [4] [5] [6]

The test oracle is explicit rather than implicit: documented assertions include element present/not present, visible/not visible, text equals/does not equal/contains/does not contain, URL checks through a URL target, and JavaScript-returns-true. Assertions also provide implicit waiting up to the element-timeout setting, avoiding arbitrary sleep steps. The official tutorial correctly warns that a flow with only actions can pass even when the business outcome failed; an assertion such as “Dashboard” is needed to make the test meaningful. This is a strong overlap with Veyra’s explicit assertion and evidence-backed outcome thesis, but Ghost Inspector’s documented abstraction is still DOM/URL/JavaScript assertions rather than business-intent contracts. [5] [7]

Ghost Inspector supports reusable modules by storing a repeated test (for example, login) as “import only” and inserting “Import steps from test” into other tests. Changes to the source module are reflected in importing tests, and multiple levels of importing are documented. Variables can hold credentials or environment-dependent values; the editor can set variables, extract values from elements or JavaScript, and the advanced product supports CSV data-driven runs across many datasets. These are reusable step sequences and parameterization, not evidence of semantic business modules with typed input/output contracts, approved rules, state transitions, or risk metadata. [8] [7] [9]

The recorder/editor covers ordinary operations (click, assign/type/select, keypress, mouse-over, drag-and-drop, pause, screenshot, navigation), file uploads, iframes, PDF rendering, date pickers, payment iframes, email flows and JavaScript. The vendor also lists visual regression (baseline screenshots, region targeting and sensitivity controls), accessibility checks based on WCAG, multiple browsers/viewport sizes/geolocations, scheduled runs as often as every minute, Slack/email/PagerDuty/webhook alerts, and screenshots/video attached to failure notifications. These claims are documented product capabilities, but the pages do not establish comparative accuracy or a hands-on benchmark. [4] [10]

## Current official pricing and trial (research date: 6 October 2026)

The official pricing page is in USD and presents both a **Monthly** and **Annual (Save 10%)** choice. The current displayed annual-commitment equivalents are Starter **$109/month billed $1,308 annually**, Growth **$225/month billed $2,700 annually**, and Large/Scale **$449/month billed $5,388 annually**. The displayed true monthly prices are Starter **$125/month**, Growth **$249/month**, and Large/Scale **$499/month**. Thus $109/$225/$449 are not true month-to-month subscriptions and should not be reported as monthly billing. Enterprise is **custom, contact for a quote**; no private contract amount is estimated here. [1]

Starter includes **10,000 test runs/month**, 5 team members, and six months of result history. Growth includes 30,000 runs/month, 15 members, and six months of result history. Large/Scale includes 100,000 runs/month, 40 members, and six months of result history. The pricing page shows email testing unavailable on Starter and available on Growth, Scale and Enterprise; SSO is shown on Scale and Enterprise, not Starter or Growth. Enterprise has custom members, SLA and priority support. These are flat workspace/plan prices, not per-user, per-test, per-minute or per-browser prices. [1]

A “test run” is one execution of one test regardless of whether it was launched in the application, API, or schedule; a suite of 10 tests consumes 10 runs even if they run simultaneously. The documentation says a single run cannot exceed 10 minutes. All plans include parallel test running, but neither the current pricing page nor the reviewed documentation states a numerical concurrency cap, so concurrency capacity is **unverified**. No separate overage rate is published: when the monthly run limit is exceeded, tests pause for the remainder of the billing period, with an email warning before the limit. The product page advertises scheduled execution and advanced integrations as features, but does not publish separate execution/minute or add-on fees. [1] [11] [4]

Every plan starts with a **14-day free trial**, with full access to Scale features and no credit card required. There is no current free tier stated on the official pricing page. Plan changes are described as upgrade-effective-immediately and downgrade-at-end-of-billing-period. The official subscription documentation says paid cancellation leaves test/suite data intact but removes previous run results, prevents execution, and preserves export capability; the page does not state a separate retention price or paid result-history add-on. [1] [11]

Older secondary listings that say $125/$249/$499 per month are consistent with the current month-to-month column, while $109/$225/$449 are the annual-commitment equivalents. Software Advice labels the prices “flat rate, per month” but does not expose the annual condition; its reviews are mostly 2017–2021 and should not be treated as current feature or price evidence. [12]

## Authoring, contracts, resilience, evidence, privacy and portability

**Recorder and intent authoring.** The recorder captures a user journey, but the documented output is operations and assertions over page elements, URLs and optional JavaScript. There is no reviewed evidence of a natural-language business-intent layer, business-event ontology, domain-specific action semantics, or an ABM connecting intent to state/dependencies/risk. The user supplies the meaning through test names, step notes and assertions. The category advantage is low-friction capture; the limitation is that recorded clicks remain vulnerable to UI/DOM changes and may not preserve the tester’s underlying business rationale.

**Assertions and outcome evidence.** Assertions can fail a test and control implicit waits. Each result documents the outcome of each step, captures a final screenshot and records a video; the product also advertises screenshots and video in failure alerts, visual diffs, accessibility violation details and result history. The official plan includes six months of result history on the three self-serve tiers. PASS/FAIL is therefore well supported; a first-class BLOCKED state, explicit evidence-quality grading, human sign-off or a structured reason taxonomy were not found in the reviewed public pages and are **unverified**, not claimed absent. [5] [1] [4]

**Reusable modules and datasets.** Imported login/flow modules are genuinely reusable and centrally updated. Variables, environment URL variables, CSV datasets and custom parameters provide parameterization. However, public documentation does not show semantic input/output schemas, preconditions/postconditions, approved rules, module version governance, impact analysis, or a business-level dependency graph. Ghost Inspector’s modules are step-subroutine reuse, not the Veyra-style semantic business module contract. [8] [9]

**Self-healing and human governance.** The product documents CSS/XPath selectors plus manually entered backup selectors. If the primary selector does not match, backup selectors are tried in order during the element timeout; the marketing page calls this “self-healing selectors.” This is deterministic fallback-selector resilience, not evidence of an ML repair proposal, a candidate diff, confidence score, approval workflow, or human-governed self-healing. The recorder itself has no site-specific semantic knowledge and the docs encourage reviewing generated selectors for durability. [4] [7]

**Diagnostics and defect memory.** Evidence includes per-step outcomes, screenshots, video, final/region screenshots, visual diffs, notes and six months of history on standard plans. The product page supplies aggregate marketing numbers (2.5M+ tests created and 54K+ sites monitored), but there is no public evidence that it learns a cross-customer defect memory, correlates recurring failures into a historical defect graph, computes risk-weighted coverage, or links defects to business capabilities. Any claim of such a moat would be analyst inference, not documented capability. [6] [1]

**Execution and privacy boundary.** Recording happens in a local browser extension, but recorded tests are synced to the Ghost Inspector service and runs occur in cloud browsers. The Chrome listing explicitly says recordings sync to the service and require an account; the tutorial says tests run from the cloud. This must not be conflated with local/offline execution or private/local AI. No AI processing or AI-based repair is documented in the reviewed official pages. The privacy policy (last modified May 25, 2018) says information may be stored/processed in the United States or other provider countries, primarily US data centers, and that User Content may be transferred in a corporate transaction; it is materially stale relative to the 2026 product changelog, so current data residency and any AI-provider boundary are **unverified**. SOC 2 is vendor-marketed/documented on the pricing and product pages, but a current audit report was not publicly reviewed. [13] [5] [14] [1]

**Portability and lock-in.** Ghost Inspector supports export to its own JSON, Selenium IDE v3 `.side`, and Selenium IDE v1 `.html`; it can import Ghost Inspector JSON and Selenium HTML. Its own JSON preserves settings, steps and imported-step references. Selenium v3 export is executable in Selenium IDE but not currently importable back into Ghost Inspector; Selenium v1 export/import bundles imported steps rather than preserving test imports. The advanced page also documents storing the simple JSON format in version control and invoking the API with JSON. This is meaningful portability and lowers lock-in relative to a closed-only recorder, but it does not export Veyra-like business semantics, evidence history, approvals, or a portable ABM. [15] [10]

## Plausible moat mechanisms and grade

**Accumulated artifacts and switching costs — emerging to moderate.** A customer can accumulate suites, reusable modules, variables, datasets, selectors, visual baselines, result history, integrations and team habits. Imported modules create dependency relationships, and six months of evidence/history plus version-controlled JSON make the system operationally sticky. The switching cost is real but limited by Selenium/JSON export, and there is no public evidence that Ghost Inspector has proprietary learned artifacts or a durable cross-customer model. Grade: **emerging/moderate**, with uncertainty about real migration pain at scale.

**Distribution and category positioning — moderate.** The browser-extension onboarding loop, “first test in under five minutes” message, broad audience (QA, engineers, product managers), free trial, public docs and built-in integrations are a coherent acquisition and adoption mechanism. The Chrome listing shows 10,000 users and 4.3/5 from 28 ratings, while the vendor claims 10,000+ teams; these are signals, not independently audited customer counts. Grade: **moderate** for distribution/support, uncertain for durability.

**Proprietary infrastructure and operating capability — emerging to moderate.** A hosted multi-browser runner, screenshots/video, visual baselines, accessibility checks, scheduling, notifications, email testing, geolocation and support across browser versions require ongoing operations. The 2026 changelog demonstrates active browser-version maintenance and enterprise-security additions. However, cloud browser execution and CI/API integrations are broadly reproducible category infrastructure, so they are not automatically a strong moat. Grade: **emerging/moderate**.

**Pricing moat — unproven to emerging.** SureSwift’s acquisition post calls the product/pricing combination a moat, but that is an investor/vendor assessment, not independent proof. Current self-serve pricing is simple flat workspace pricing with large run quotas and no published overage meter; it can be attractive for high-volume teams, but competitors can copy price structures. Grade: **unproven/emerging**.

**Semantic intelligence or AI moat — unproven.** No reviewed public source documents an application behavior model, cross-customer learned failure memory, autonomous exploration, AI locator repair, or human-approved AI suggestions. Generic LLM access and feature count are not moats. Grade: **unproven**.

## Supported limitations, inference and Veyra fit/risk

Documented constraints include cloud execution, a ten-minute maximum per run, monthly run quotas that pause execution when exhausted, and reliance on account/service access for recording and running. The official login guidance warns that CAPTCHA and authenticator-app 2FA are not supported in the basic module tutorial, although the advanced marketing page says “2FA logins and more” can be handled; this is a material internal inconsistency, so exact 2FA coverage should be treated as **unverified** rather than declared absent. The public docs also recommend dummy/staging credentials and warn against using private production credentials. [8] [11] [10]

Independent verified-user reviews on Software Advice report practical limitations: dynamic sites can cause frequent breakage and maintenance; cloud execution prevents local-environment runs; some reviewers found no step-by-step debugging; older reviews mention limitations in complex flow control. These are dated user reports, not a current hands-on benchmark, and the current product now documents JavaScript/conditions, backup selectors and advanced scenarios. They are useful risk signals rather than definitive current feature absences. [12]

For Veyra, Ghost Inspector validates that there is durable demand for “manual flow to reusable test” and that explicit assertions, reusable login-like modules, screenshots/video and human-readable results are commercially legible. Its gap relative to Veyra’s thesis is not basic recording: Ghost Inspector already covers that baseline strongly, including cloud scale, schedules, cross-browser execution and CI/API. Veyra should therefore avoid positioning “record clicks and replay them” or generic assertions/modules as a moat.

Veyra’s plausible differentiation is a local-first, manual/low-code workflow with explicit Teach → Test → Results separation; semantic business modules with typed parameters and contracts; evidence-backed PASS/FAIL/BLOCKED; an ABM connecting intent, states, dependencies, approved rules, failure history and risk; and human-governed AI suggestions. Those are opportunity hypotheses, not proven advantages. They matter especially for teams that cannot send sensitive workflows to a cloud runner, need local/private staging access, need a first-class BLOCKED outcome, or need business-readable evidence rather than DOM-step replay. Conversely, Ghost Inspector’s mature browser matrix, scheduling, parallelism, cloud operations, API/CI, visual/accessibility/email testing and 2026 browser maintenance expose Veyra’s V0 exclusions as meaningful gaps, not immediate differentiators: Veyra currently excludes multi-browser, CI, scheduling, parallel runs, API testing and autonomous exploration. Ghost Inspector’s portability and no-code adoption also reduce the strength of a simple local-vs-cloud switching-cost argument.

## Material gaps and evidence caveats

The public sources do not disclose numerical parallel concurrency limits, per-run compute capacity beyond the ten-minute maximum, data-center selection by plan, current subprocessor/AI-provider details, current data retention for screenshots/video beyond the six-month plan statement, a current privacy-policy revision, defect-memory/coverage algorithms, AI/self-healing approval controls, or a supported offline/private cloud runner. Missing public documentation is not evidence that the capability is absent. The pricing page contains both monthly and annual values in extracted text; the billing labels make the distinction clear, but a buyer should confirm the checkout display at purchase. The acquisition history is clear from SureSwift’s 2022 announcement, while current ownership beyond the Ghost Inspector/SureSwift relationship was not independently re-established; the live docs, site and extension show the Ghost Inspector brand remains active.
