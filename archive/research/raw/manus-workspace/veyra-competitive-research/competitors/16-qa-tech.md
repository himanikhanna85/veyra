# QA.tech — competitive profile

**Research date: 6 October 2026.** This is a desk-research profile of publicly available material; it is not a hands-on benchmark. “Vendor-marketed” means a QA.tech claim; “documented capability” means described in QA.tech documentation; “independently supported” means corroborated by the cited third-party review; “assessment” is analyst inference. Missing public documentation is treated as **unverified**, not as proof of absence.

## 1. Active identity, status and intended customer

QA.tech is an active Stockholm, Sweden software company founded in 2023, positioning itself as an AI-driven/agentic QA platform for engineering teams shipping web and native mobile applications. Its stated mission is to remove the “testing tax” from developers; the current home page targets teams building quickly with AI and describes agents validating changes from pull request to production. The company says it has $5 million in funding and names customers or logos including Lovable, AstraZeneca, Upsales and AdminPulse. These funding and customer assertions are vendor claims, not independently audited here. [1]

The product is now broader than the original “AI exploratory browser tester” framing: current pages advertise web, mobile, API, voice, PR, regression, accessibility, localization and multi-application flows. For this comparison, the material differentiator is still autonomous, intent-driven browser/UI exploration: the user states a goal in natural language; an agent crawls and reasons through the rendered application like a person, rather than replaying a selector script. QA.tech’s intended customer is therefore a software product/engineering or QA team with a reachable test environment, active releases and a CI/CD workflow—not primarily a standalone manual tester working locally. [1][2]

The company’s October 2025 “QA.tech 1.0” post described browser-based learning, documentation context, change history and a knowledge graph; the 2026 docs and product pages show an expanded operational platform with test drafts, plans, API/CLI/MCP access, native mobile and PR review. I found no evidence of a corporate acquisition or rebrand in the reviewed official sources. The current identity remains QA.tech; older third-party pricing pages should not be treated as current list pricing.

## 2. Distinctive capabilities and mechanism

**Goal/intent-driven agent execution.** QA.tech asks users to describe a user journey and expected outcome in plain English, then generates steps and drives a real browser. Its documented test model separates an action-oriented **goal** from an observable **expected result**; the agent can adapt its actions as the rendered UI changes. This is distinctive versus the category baseline of hand-authored Playwright/Cypress/Selenium steps, but it is not exclusive: other AI testing products also market goal-based execution. [3][4]

**Dynamic PR testing.** The most differentiated workflow is change-aware testing at the pull-request boundary. QA.tech says it classifies a PR, connects to a preview environment, selects relevant regression and exploratory work, and posts a verdict into GitHub or GitLab. It explicitly distinguishes pre-existing failures from failures attributable to the diff and returns **unverified** when a run did not exercise the change, rather than silently treating that as green. That is a strong product thesis around evidence quality, although the mechanism and accuracy are vendor-documented, not independently benchmarked. [5]

**Exploration beyond fixed cases.** QA.tech markets agents that crawl the app, build a feature/knowledge graph, explore edge cases and maintain a coverage map of untested flows. The official documentation supports a knowledge graph used for semantic search during test creation and a project summary/context used at execution; it does not expose enough public detail to validate graph completeness, prioritization quality or defect yield. [4][6]

**Failure reconstruction and rich evidence.** Official materials document a video/screen recording, step trace, screenshots, console and network activity, agent reasoning/context at failure, and structured results/issues that can be sent to Jira or Linear. The API documentation index additionally exposes run-test-case history, evaluator assessment, screenshots, logs, trace and deduplicated issues. This is materially more diagnostic than a bare pass/fail, but the quality of root-cause explanations remains unverified without a trial. [2][5][7]

**Conversational authoring plus human approval.** Chat can generate multiple tests from a request, ticket, PR, uploaded PDF/image/text specification, or existing checklist. Generated tests are drafts and are not committed to the project until the user selects/adds them; tests then remain Draft until reviewed and activated. This is a genuine human-governance control around AI-authored coverage. It is not the same as a formal approval workflow for every agent adaptation during execution. [3][8]

**Cross-surface journeys.** QA.tech now claims one scenario can span web, mobile and API, with state carried across steps; this is outside Veyra V0 and reflects a broader platform ambition. API, mobile and voice are paid-tier features on the current plan matrix rather than evidence that every plan has them. [2]

## 3. Current official pricing and commercial terms

As of the research date, QA.tech’s official pricing page lists three tiers—**Starter, Growth and Enterprise**—but displays **no currency, dollar/euro amount, monthly price, annual price, per-user price, per-test price, per-execution price, per-minute rate, or published overage rate**. Every tier directs the buyer to “Talk to us.” Therefore the current official commercial posture is **quote-based for all tiers**; private contract prices must not be estimated. [2]

Starter is described for individuals/small teams and includes 1 user, 3 parallel test runs, standard model access and 2 environments. Growth includes up to 5 users, custom parallel runs, standard model access and a custom number of environments. Enterprise has custom users, custom parallel runs, a fine-tuned-model option and unlimited environments. The page says test creation, chat and maintenance are included and only test executions count under a fair-usage policy, but it publishes no execution quota, included run count, minutes, token/credit unit, concurrency price, or overage schedule. “Custom parallel test runs” is a capacity term, not a published price unit. [2]

Feature gating is explicit: Starter includes web and exploratory testing, unlimited test creation, spending limits/budget alerts, 30-day data retention and email support; it excludes mobile, API, voice, dynamic PR testing, multi-application flows, scheduled plans, coverage/performance reports and MCP/API/CLI access. Growth adds mobile/API/voice, dynamic PR, geo/i18n, multi-application flows, scheduled plans, coverage/performance reports, 90-day retention, built-in integrations, MCP/API/CLI, custom MCP, guided POC and shared Slack/Teams. Enterprise adds custom retention, unlimited environments, quicker runs, fine-tuned model option, SSO/enforced MFA, SSH tunnels/private network access, dedicated support, SLA, custom security review and contract/payment terms. [2]

The official FAQ says QA.tech offers a POC starting with 2–3 critical journeys and longer pilots for committed teams; this is a sales-led evaluation, not a publicly priced free trial. No self-serve trial length or free plan is documented. The page says execution usage is metered under fair use, but does not define what constitutes an execution or how limits/overages are enforced. Annual-vs-monthly billing is not disclosed; do not infer a monthly subscription from the tier names. [2]

A 2024 third-party listing claimed a $99/month team plan and $89/month/developer professional plan, but the current official page has replaced numeric pricing with quote-based Starter/Growth/Enterprise and does not confirm those figures. Retain this only as historical, contradicted evidence—not current list pricing. [9]

## 4. Capability and architecture assessment

**Recorder and intent authoring:** QA.tech explicitly says “no scripts, no recording” for web authoring; users write goals in chat or UI and the agent generates steps. A traditional recorder is therefore not documented. Existing Playwright/Cypress cases can reportedly be brought over, but the public sources do not establish the exact import format or fidelity. [4]

**Assertions/oracle:** The documented primitive is goal plus expected result, with observable end-state examples such as a success message, redirect or email. The PR page describes pass/fail and unverified verdicts. This is an outcome oracle, not a public schema for typed assertions, explicit business rules, tolerances, invariants or evidence-linked assertion objects. Whether users can author multiple intermediate assertions or formal contracts is **unverified**. [3][5]

**Reusable modules/semantic contracts:** QA.tech supports dependencies and “Resume From” browser state, shared steps via dependencies, Agent Rules, or assistant docs, scenarios, labels, project/application/environment organization and persistent knowledge. Agent Rules can be targeted by application, label, scenario or URL path. This resembles reusable semantic procedures and contextual rules, but I found no public evidence of parameterized business modules with typed inputs/outputs, versioned semantic contracts, or a domain ABM connecting business intent, states, dependencies, approved rules and risk. [6][10]

**Datasets and secrets:** Configs hold credentials, generated emails, API keys, custom fields, files and SMS/email inputs; environment overrides allow the same config to use different values by environment. However, the documentation warns config data is stored unencrypted and passed to the model during a run, recommending dedicated least-privilege test accounts. This is useful parameterization but is materially different from a local-first, privacy-preserving dataset vault. [11]

**Self-healing and approval controls:** QA.tech’s mechanism is agent adaptation to rendered UI and intent rather than selector repair; vendor language calls this tests surviving redesigns. New tests are drafts and require explicit activation, which is documented human governance. I found no public control requiring human approval before an agent changes an already-active test’s behavior, and no public diff/approval log for autonomous adaptation. Treat “self-healing with approval” as **unverified**. [4][8]

**Diagnostics, evidence and history:** Screenshots, videos, traces, console/network logs, evaluator summaries, issue deduplication and run history are documented. Revision history snapshots goal, expected result, steps, configs, start path, agent, device preset and owner; test-case history supports pass/fail/duration analysis. The platform also markets coverage reports/maps and historical product knowledge. It does not publicly specify defect-memory semantics, confidence calibration, evidence retention beyond tier-level 30/90/custom days, or whether coverage is business-risk weighted. [2][7][8]

**Local execution versus cloud AI:** QA.tech states agents and test infrastructure run entirely in its secure cloud. Its CLI can create a tunnel from a local port to the cloud runner, allowing a local dev server to be tested, but that is not offline execution and does not make AI processing local/private. Enterprise SSH tunnels/private network access are available; the public docs do not establish an on-device browser/LLM mode. [1][12]

**Privacy/security:** QA.tech claims SOC 2 Type 2, SSO/SAML on suitable tiers, and that customer data is not used to train models. The Trust Center publicly lists Google Cloud Platform, Supabase, Anthropic and OpenAI as subprocessors/engineering providers and says the SOC 2 Type 2 report is available on request. This supports a cloud enterprise-security posture, not local-first privacy. Config documentation’s unencrypted-storage/model-passing warning is a notable operational caveat. [1][11][13]

**Portability/lock-in:** QA.tech’s pricing page claims “test definitions stay portable and open,” while the independent review says tests cannot be exported as code-based scripts. These are not necessarily contradictory—portable definitions may mean QA.tech-native data rather than Playwright code—but exact export formats are not publicly confirmed. The product has switching costs in knowledge graph/context, agent rules, configs, dependencies, historical runs and integrations; portability should therefore be treated as vendor-claimed/unverified until an export is inspected. [2][7]

## 5. Plausible moat mechanisms

**Accumulated learned artifacts — emerging, with uncertainty.** The knowledge graph, project summary, memories, rules, crawling data, test revisions, dependency structure, run history and coverage map can compound application-specific context. This could improve test generation and reduce onboarding friction. It is not a proven data-network effect: the public evidence does not quantify learning quality, cross-customer transfer, or whether artifacts are exportable and useful outside QA.tech. [6][8]

**Switching costs — moderate, but not necessarily durable.** Once a team encodes tests, configs, environment overrides, rules, dependencies, tickets, CI gates and historical evidence, migration costs rise. QA.tech’s vendor portability claim could reduce this moat, while the lack of confirmed code export could increase it. Grade: moderate with unresolved portability risk. [2][7][11]

**Distribution/workflow embedding — emerging to moderate.** GitHub/GitLab PR checks, CI/CD integrations, Jira/Linear issue creation, MCP/API/CLI and guided POCs place QA.tech in the engineering release loop. This can create habitual use and operational data, but the integrations are available in competing categories and do not by themselves establish defensibility. [2][5]

**Proprietary infrastructure/operating capability — emerging.** Cloud-isolated browser/mobile/API runners, visual agent execution, evidence capture, change-to-test selection and enterprise network/security operations are technically and operationally non-trivial. Still, QA.tech’s public pages do not disclose architecture, reliability, unit economics, evaluation data or unique model training; the moat is therefore unproven rather than strong. Funding and generic LLM access are not counted as moats.

**Overall moat: emerging/moderate, not established.** The most plausible mechanism is the compound combination of application-specific knowledge plus release-workflow embedding and historical evidence. It could become strong if the system demonstrably improves coverage/defect discovery and makes migration painful, but current public evidence is vendor-marketed and lacks independent longitudinal validation.

## 6. Limitations, risks and fit for Veyra

Supported limitations include **Chromium-only web execution** (with custom viewports for mobile web), cloud execution rather than offline/local AI, no published self-serve numeric pricing, and a sales-led POC/quote process. An independent 2026 review additionally reports limited cross-browser coverage beyond Chromium and inability to export tests as code-based scripts; its review methodology and claims are not a hands-on benchmark available to this research, so treat those as independent review evidence rather than definitive product specification. [4][7]

The current product materially exceeds Veyra V0’s scope—API, multi-surface mobile, CI/PR testing, scheduling, parallel runs and autonomous exploration are all advertised or plan-gated—so it is a direct future competitor in autonomous browser QA but not a like-for-like V0 substitute. QA.tech’s public material does **not** prove explicit PASS/FAIL/BLOCKED evidence semantics as Veyra defines them; it does document pass/fail and unverified, while BLOCKED handling, human adjudication, and evidence-linked assertions are **unverified**. [2][5]

For Veyra, QA.tech validates that plain-language intent, persistent product context, visual agent execution, useful diagnostics and change-aware coverage are real market needs. It also exposes openings: local-first browser execution and private/on-device processing; manual/low-code tester ownership rather than engineering/CI-first adoption; explicit evidence-backed PASS/FAIL/BLOCKED with visible assertion oracles; parameterized reusable semantic business modules with typed contracts; human approval for AI suggestions and adaptations; and transparent, predictable pricing. These are analyst assessments based on the gap between QA.tech’s documented cloud-agent model and Veyra’s thesis—not proof that QA.tech cannot add them.

QA.tech’s strongest competitive threat to Veyra is its mature autonomous loop and release-workflow distribution, plus its application memory and evidence stack. Veyra should avoid competing on “AI browser testing” alone. A defensible wedge would be trustworthy human-governed evidence, local/private operation, business-semantic reusable modules, and a manual tester experience that remains useful when autonomous exploration is disabled. The ABM remains a thesis: QA.tech’s knowledge graph is adjacent, but not evidence of an equivalent business-behaviour model.

## 7. Material evidence gaps

Public sources do not disclose numeric prices, billing cadence, included execution quotas, overages, minute/token units, or concurrency charges. They do not provide a public export file/schema, hands-on reliability/false-positive rates, independent defect-yield study, detailed browser/device matrix beyond Chromium plus native mobile claims, offline/private AI mode, typed assertion/contract schema, approval audit trail for adaptive active tests, BLOCKED semantics, or details of model routing/evaluation. The pages are marketing/documentation, not a product benchmark; no account, trial, purchase or interactive test was performed.
